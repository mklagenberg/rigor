## Scope and responsibility model

The original question is broader than one causal claim. This run distinguishes:

1. **knowledge and monitoring** — awareness of Brazilian conspirators and events;
2. **political alignment and encouragement** — preference for, contact with, or support to anti-Goulart forces;
3. **material preparation** — plans for fuel, arms, naval, air, or financial support;
4. **operational intervention** — material actually delivered or U.S. forces directly used to determine the outcome;
5. **causal responsibility for the 1964 overthrow** — contribution to the fall of João Goulart;
6. **responsibility for the 1964–1985 dictatorship as a whole** — support, influence, or participation across later governments and specific acts.

The collected evidence is strongest for categories 1–3 and political support immediately after the overthrow. It is insufficient to assign the United States sole or primary causal responsibility for the coup, and insufficient to generalize the same degree of responsibility to every phase and act of the 21-year dictatorship.

## Chronology

- **26–28 March 1964:** U.S. Embassy reporting described organized Brazilian military opposition; Gordon recommended clandestine and overt contingency support.
- **31 March:** Brazilian military action began. Senior U.S. officials reviewed political, military, logistical, and economic responses.
- **31 March–1 April:** Operation Brother Sam instructions placed naval, fuel, airlift, and other support on standby under stated conditions.
- **Early April:** Goulart left power before the planned U.S. force could determine the conflict militarily.
- **10 April:** the embassy criticized aspects of the Institutional Act while recommending political and economic support for the successor government.

## Provenance and interest analysis

- FRUS documents are U.S. government records selected and edited by the State Department's historical series. They are strong evidence of what U.S. officials wrote and planned, but publication selection and remaining redactions must be recorded.
- An ambassador's analysis of Goulart or Brazilian society is a situated source claim, not neutral fact.
- The Brazilian CNV had a statutory mandate to investigate serious human-rights violations. Its findings require chapter- and document-level citation when used for specific responsibility claims.
- Cold War interests shaped U.S. interpretations; Brazilian political, military, economic, media, and civil-society actors had their own interests and agency.

## Challenge pass

### Alternative: “The United States merely observed events”

Rejected. The primary records document recommendations for covert support, preparation for material and overt assistance, and support for the successor government.

### Alternative: “The United States created and executed the coup”

Not established. Brazilian forces initiated and carried out the overthrow. The contingency support was prepared, but the collected evidence does not show U.S. combat forces determined the result.

### Alternative: “No deployed intervention means no responsibility”

Rejected as too narrow. Political encouragement, covert activity, contingency preparation, recognition, and post-coup support are relevant forms of responsibility even without direct combat.

## Conclusion

**Judgment:** Yes, the United States bears documented responsibility as an external political supporter and prepared material enabler of the 1964 overthrow, and as an early supporter of the successor government. This responsibility is significant but not exclusive: Brazilian actors planned, initiated, and executed the coup. The evidence reviewed here does not support saying that the United States single-handedly caused the dictatorship, nor does it by itself allocate U.S. responsibility for every later action from 1964 to 1985.

## Remediation

A reference-grade dossier on the entire dictatorship must add separate workstreams for:

- U.S.–Brazil relations under each military administration;
- police and military training, intelligence exchange, and security doctrine;
- economic assistance, arms, and diplomatic responses;
- U.S. knowledge of and response to documented violations;
- Brazilian primary archives and scholarship independent of U.S. custody;
- surviving redactions, missing records, and conflicting historiography.
