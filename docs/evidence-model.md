# Evidence model

## Claim types

Every substantive statement is one of:

- **Observation:** directly recorded event, document, measurement, or quotation.
- **Source claim:** what a source says; it is not yet accepted as fact.
- **Derived fact:** reproducible transformation of defined inputs.
- **Inference:** a conclusion that follows with stated assumptions.
- **Judgment:** a recommendation, evaluation, or interpretation.
- **Open question:** unresolved point that could alter the conclusion.

Final reports MUST preserve these distinctions.

## Hypotheses and missing evidence

A hypothesis is a distinct working object, not a claim type. It records a testable explanation, alternatives, expected traces, disconfirming evidence, and current status. It may direct search, calculation, scenario, and challenge work, but it MUST NOT upgrade an inference or expected trace into evidence.

For a missing record, distinguish: record absence, record loss, access/indexing failure, inadequate identity coverage, and evidence that contradicts the hypothesis. “Not found” is not a finding of nonexistence without a coverage assessment.

See [hypotheses and research loops](hypotheses-and-research-loops.md).

## Evidence lineage

A lineage traces a material claim back to its origin. Record:

- claim identifier and wording;
- source or derivation identifier;
- original publication or dataset where available;
- publication and access date;
- source type and directness;
- independence group;
- method, sample, denominator, and transformation;
- source incentives or conflicts when material;
- support, conflict, and limitation notes.

Two sources are not independent if they rely on the same origin, dataset, press release, funding body, interview, model output, or unverified repost.

## Source assessment

Assess sources on six separate dimensions:

1. **Provenance:** who created it, when, and from what access?
2. **Competence:** does the source have relevant knowledge or method?
3. **Directness:** how close is it to the event, data, or decision?
4. **Independence:** is its evidence lineage distinct?
5. **Incentives:** what could systematically shape its account?
6. **Fitness:** does the evidence actually answer this claim at this time?

An official source can be authoritative but incomplete, selectively framed, stale, or self-interested. A community source can be valuable first-hand evidence but still need identity, scope, and corroboration checks.

## Quantitative checks

For every material numeric claim, inspect definition, unit, numerator, denominator, period, population, sample selection, missingness, aggregation, baseline, chart scale, and whether a percentage point is being confused with a percent change.

A chart is an argument, not a neutral container. Preserve the underlying table or a reproducible extraction when feasible.

## Follow the money

When incentives are material, map ownership, funding, paid promotion, affiliate links, procurement relationships, lobbying, sponsorship, regulatory incentives, and who benefits if the claim is accepted. This is context for interpretation, not proof of dishonesty.

## Confidence

Confidence belongs to a claim, not to the author. It reflects evidence quality, directness, independence, consistency, freshness, and the strength of credible alternatives. Use qualitative labels only with a short rationale.
